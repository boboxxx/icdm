\begin{abstract}
We present a blind disturbance-model selector for diffusion-aided semantic
image reception. Given a received latent, it selects Gaussian denoising or
joint signal--interference reconstruction and decodes the recovered latent
into an image. Rather than reconstructing an unknown interferer on every
frame, the selector uses received energy and block-energy heterogeneity to
choose one receiver before diffusion inference. The rule requires only
$O(n)$ calculations and no neural retraining; its thresholds are fitted on
development images and frozen before evaluation. Experiments use CelebA
images with CIFAR-10 interference and identical observations across receivers.
\input{generated/abstract_result.tex}
An additional same-gate control tests whether the gain extends beyond bypassing
joint inference; separately trained DeepJSCC and MambaJSCC codecs provide
external references under the same channel-use budget.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Semantic image communication requires recovering image content from limited,
corrupted channel observations. Deep joint source--channel coding (JSCC)
addresses this task by mapping images directly to channel symbols, with
gradual quality degradation as noise increases \cite{bourtsoulatze2019deep}.
SwinJSCC and MambaJSCC extend this approach through transformer and state-space
architectures \cite{yang2025swinjscc,wu2024mambajscc}. Generative receivers
further exploit learned priors to recover corrupted representations. Even so,
co-channel interference of unknown power and structure leaves a deployed
receiver with a choice: approximate the disturbance as noise or reconstruct
the interfering signal explicitly.

CDDM and ICDM provide two relevant recovery mechanisms. CDDM matches the
aggregate disturbance to a diffusion noise level \cite{wu2024cddm}, whereas
ICDM jointly reconstructs desired and interfering latents \cite{wu2026icdm}.
Iterative source error correction \cite{pmlr-v206-lee23c}, channel-conditioned
diffusion reconstruction \cite{wang2025diffcom}, and blind inverse-problem
inference \cite{murata2023gibbsddrm} also advance receiver adaptation. However,
joint reconstruction must infer two latents and unknown interference
amplitudes from one mixture. Estimation errors can offset the benefit of
modeling interference structure while consuming additional computation.
Gaussian denoising avoids that estimation burden but discards useful
structure. This raises the question: can the receiver choose between these
models from the observation alone, before paying for generative inference?

In this paper, we take a step toward observation-dependent generative
reception. We introduce a blind disturbance-model selector that takes a
received latent and chooses either Gaussian denoising or joint reconstruction.
The selected receiver recovers the desired latent for a common image decoder.
The selector computes two energy statistics in $O(n)$ time and executes only
one branch. All neural weights remain frozen. This design tests whether
changing the receiver decision alone can improve image recovery, without
changing the transmitted representation or training new neural modules.

Our key observation is that explicit interference modeling offers structure
at the cost of estimation uncertainty. Its value can therefore vary across
received frames. To inform this choice, we use excess received energy to
measure aggregate disturbance and block-energy heterogeneity to describe its
variation across latent blocks. Neither statistic identifies the
interfering image; desired-signal variation also affects both. We therefore
use image-group validation to compare energy-only and two-feature rules on
separate development images, retaining a fixed receiver if adaptation gives
no validation gain. The chosen rule is frozen before independent confirmation.

Experiments on 256 independent image pairs show a mean PSNR gain of 0.750 dB
over blind Gaussian denoising and 1.334 dB over calibrated joint sampling,
with 34.7\% fewer predictor evaluations than joint sampling. These gains require
no neural retraining. A same-gate control tests whether selection adds value
beyond bypassing joint inference. Profile-wise comparisons reveal that the
preferred fixed receiver changes with interference structure, while harmful
decisions and block mismatch delimit the benefit. Separately trained DeepJSCC
and MambaJSCC provide external codec references. Our shared-prior receiver
ports do not reproduce the complete published CDDM or ICDM systems.

Our contributions are threefold. (1) We introduce a blind selector that
chooses a disturbance model before generative inference using two energy
statistics. (2) We show that the preferred fixed receiver differs across
interference profiles, motivating observation-dependent selection.
(3) We establish a mean-quality and computation benefit on independent paired
observations, while quantifying harmful decisions and mismatch sensitivity.
Section~\ref{sec:method} develops the method, Section~\ref{sec:experiments}
reports experiments, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the findings
and concludes the paper.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
In this section, we develop a blind selector that maps a received latent to
one of two generative receivers. We first define the communication model,
recovery models, and selection objective, then describe the candidate
receivers and the rule fitted on development images.

\subsection{Communication Model}
Let $\bm u\in[0,1]^d$ be an RGB image, with $d=3H_{\rm img}W_{\rm img}$.
The JSCC encoder $f_s$ combines representation learning and channel mapping;
$f_z$ encodes an interfering image $\bm v$ into the same dimension:
\begin{equation}
 \bm x=\mathcal N(f_s(\bm u)),\qquad
 \bm z=\mathcal N(f_z(\bm v)),\quad
 \mathcal N(\bm t)=\frac{\sqrt n\,\bm t}{\norm{\bm t}}.
 \label{eq:encode}
\end{equation}
Each nonzero codeword has unit average power and bandwidth ratio $n/d$. For $B$
blocks of lengths $L_b$, with $\sum_bL_b=n$, the received symbols are
\begin{equation}
 \bm y_b=\bm x_b+a_b\bm z_b+\bm w_b,\qquad
 \bm w\sim\mathcal{CN}(\bm0,N_0\bm I),\quad a_b\ge0 .
 \label{eq:channel}
\end{equation}
Here $a_b$ is the unknown interference amplitude and $N_0$ the complex noise
power. Desired-channel gain is unity; symbol alignment is known.
The receiver knows $N_0$, the normalization, and block
boundaries, but not $\bm u$, $\bm z$, or $a_b$. For nominal SINR $\gamma$, the
simulator sets the average interference power to $10^{-\gamma/10}-N_0$.
Realized block energies of $\bm z$ make the per-frame SINR differ from this
nominal setting.

\subsection{Generative Image Recovery}
The receiver combines the frame-specific evidence in $\bm y$ with learned
latent priors. Joint reconstruction uses both priors:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{split}
 p(\bm x,\bm z\mid\bm y,\bm a)&\propto p_s(\bm x)p_z(\bm z)\\
 &\quad\times\exp\!\left[-\frac{1}{N_0}\sum_b
 \norm{\bm y_b-\bm x_b-a_b\bm z_b}^{2}\right].
 \end{split}
 \label{eq:joint}
\end{equation}
The amplitudes must be estimated. Moreover, a matching mixture cannot identify
both latents: $(\bm x_b+a_b\bm\delta_b,\bm z_b-\bm\delta_b)$ produces the same
observation. Gaussian recovery instead uses only $p_s$:
\begin{equation}
 p_{\G}(\bm y\mid\bm x;\nu)=
 \mathcal{CN}(\bm y;\bm x,\nu\bm I),\qquad \nu\ge N_0 .
 \label{eq:gaussian}
\end{equation}
The Gaussian and joint receivers, $\mathcal R_{\G}$ and $\mathcal R_{\SIC}$,
use frozen diffusion priors with observation-based initialization or guidance.
Neither performs exact posterior sampling, and only joint recovery estimates a
second latent. A blind rule $\pi_\theta(\bm y)\in\{\G,\SIC\}$ selects one branch.
The common decoder then reconstructs
\begin{equation}
 \widehat{\bm u}_\theta
 =g_s\!\left(\mathcal N\!\left(\mathcal R_{\pi_\theta(\bm y)}(\bm y)\right)\right).
 \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Receiver-Selection Objective}
We measure image reconstruction with per-image PSNR, $q(\bm u,\widehat{\bm u})=
-10\log_{10}(\|\bm u-\widehat{\bm u}\|^2/d)$ for clipped RGB outputs.
LPIPS and MS-SSIM measure fidelity, not downstream accuracy. Computation counts
calls to both priors; receiver latency includes selection and decoding.

For development pairs $\mathcal D=\{(\bm u_i,\bm y_i)\}_{i=1}^{M}$, we fit
\begin{equation}
 \theta^\star\in\arg\max_{\theta\in\Theta}
 \frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}q(\bm u_i,\widehat{\bm u}_\theta(\bm y_i)),
 \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
where $\Theta$ contains the rule family retained by image-group validation and
a constant-receiver fallback. Bandwidth, power, and neural weights remain
fixed; computation is reported separately. Fitting is offline; at inference,
the frozen rule executes one branch without knowing its reconstruction error.

\subsection{Candidate Receivers}
Both receivers share the frozen desired-signal prior and decoder, isolating
the disturbance-model choice. To account for unknown interference power, the
Gaussian receiver estimates aggregate disturbance from the received symbols:
\begin{equation}
 \begin{gathered}
 e_b=\frac{\norm{\bm y_b}^2}{L_b},\qquad
 \bar e=\frac{1}{n}\sum_b L_be_b,\\
 \widehat P=\pos{\bar e-1-N_0},\qquad
 \widehat\nu=N_0+\widehat P.
 \end{gathered}
 \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
With unit source power and a vanishing signal--interference cross expectation,
unclipped excess energy estimates the mean interference energy. Finite blocks,
latent correlation, and clipping make this estimate inexact. In particular,
local desired-signal energy can resemble interference heterogeneity.

Let $r(\bm y)\in\R^{2n}$ stack real and imaginary components. Given the training
diffusion schedule $\bar\alpha_t$, the Gaussian receiver $\G$ starts at the
nearest noise level satisfying
\begin{equation}
 \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t_0}}{\bar\alpha_{t_0}}
 \simeq\frac{\widehat\nu}{2},\qquad
 \bm v_{t_0}=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t_0}}\,r(\bm y).
 \label{eq:match}
\end{equation}
The factor $1/2$ converts complex noise power to variance per real coordinate.
For decreasing respaced indices, a noise predictor $\epsilon_s$ gives
\begin{align}
 \widehat{\bm x}_0&=
 \frac{\bm v_t-\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\epsilon_s(\bm v_t,t)}
 {\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}},\\
 \bm v_{t'}&=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t'}}\widehat{\bm x}_0+
 \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t'}}\epsilon_s(\bm v_t,t).
 \label{eq:gaussian_update}
\end{align}
The receiver uses at most 40 predictor evaluations and fewer when the matched
initial index is below 39.

To exploit interference structure, the joint receiver $\SIC$ initializes
$a_b^{(0)}=\sqrt{\pos{e_b-1-N_0}}$ and performs joint diffusion inference with
signal and interference predictors. During late corrector updates it obtains
denoised, frame-normalized estimates $\widetilde{\bm x}$ and
$\widetilde{\bm z}$, then updates
\begin{equation}
 a_b^{+}=\pos{\frac{
 \Re\{\widetilde{\bm z}_b^{H}
       (\bm y_b-\widetilde{\bm x}_b)\}}
 {\norm{\widetilde{\bm z}_b}^{2}}}.
 \label{eq:amplitude}
\end{equation}
An empty denominator retains the previous amplitude. This nonnegative
least-squares update is conditional on point estimates and does not correct
their uncertainty. Calibration begins when the signal coefficient
$\alpha(t)\ge0.5$. Estimated block SINR updates the inherited guidance
coefficients by clipped linear interpolation of the original AWGN table.
The existing 40-step, order-three sampler calls each prior 40 times. An
initialization-only variant keeps the amplitudes fixed and isolates the effect
of calibration.

\subsection{Blind Selection Before Inference}
To distinguish frames with similar total energy but different block profiles,
the selector combines $\widehat P$ with normalized block-energy variation:
\begin{equation}
 H=\frac{B^{-1}\sum_{b=1}^{B}(e_b-\bar e)^2}
          {\max(\bar e^2,\varepsilon)}.
 \label{eq:heterogeneity}
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon>0$ prevents division by zero, and all receiver blocks have
equal length. The statistic $H$ measures energy variation, not the interference
distribution. For $\theta=(\tau_\ell,\tau_h,\eta)$, the rule is
\begin{equation}
 \pi_\theta(\bm y)=
 \begin{cases}
 \G,&\widehat P\le \tau_\ell,\ H\le\eta,\\
 \G,&\widehat P\le \tau_h,\ H>\eta,\\
 \SIC,&\text{otherwise}.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:selector}
\end{equation}
We also test the reversed assignment of $\G$ and $\SIC$ in
\eqref{eq:selector}; thus, the Gaussian branch is not assigned to the low-power
region a priori. The energy-only family sets $\tau_\ell=\tau_h$. Either family
can reduce to a constant receiver, allowing validation to reject adaptation.

To assess transfer across images, eight development folds keep each image's
conditions together. We fit thresholds on seven folds and evaluate PSNR on
the eighth, then refit the best out-of-fold family on all development images.
Ties favor lower cost, then the simpler family. If neither
family improves on the better fixed receiver, validation retains that receiver.
Confirmation and pressure results never alter the thresholds. At inference,
the selector evaluates \eqref{eq:selector} and executes only the chosen branch.
This adds $O(n)$ energy calculations and no diffusion evaluations.

For analysis, let $q_j(\bm u,\bm y)$ be a candidate's PSNR. The unavailable
per-frame oracle has quality $\max(q_\G,q_\SIC)$. The exact identity
\begin{equation}
 \max(q_\G,q_\SIC)-q_{\pi_\theta}
 =|q_\G-q_\SIC|\,
 \mathbf1\{\pi_\theta\ne\arg\max_j q_j\}
 \label{eq:regret}
\end{equation}
shows that complementary receivers alone are insufficient: errors on large-gap
frames dominate regret. This identity is an accounting relation, not a
generalization bound. The fitting objective is mean PSNR; it does not optimize
mean MSE, LPIPS, or harmful-tail probability.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
In this section, we test whether selecting one frozen receiver improves image
quality while reducing computation. We compare fixed receivers and external
codecs, then examine harmful decisions and interference mismatch.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
We use $128\times128$ CelebA center crops as desired images
\cite{liu2015celeba};
$32\times32$ CIFAR-10 interference images are nearest-neighbor upsampled.
Training uses 162,770 desired and 50,000 interference images. The frozen
SwinJSCC codec has stage widths $(128,192,256)$, depths $(2,2,6)$, and an
$8\times16\times16$ real latent: 1,024 complex symbols at CBR $1/48$.
Each DiT prior has 28 blocks, width 1,152, 16 heads and patch size two.
The inherited training schedule uses 40/100 desired/interference-codec epochs
and 50/20 desired/interference-prior epochs; selection adds no neural training.

DeepJSCC and MambaJSCC each train desired and interference codecs for 40 epochs
at SNR 20 dB and CBR $1/48$, with one seed and validation-MSE checkpoint
selection. DeepJSCC uses a pinned third-party PyTorch reproduction; MambaJSCC
uses official code. Public checkpoints also provide transfer diagnostics
without fine-tuning, using one encoder for both streams. Public MambaJSCC
differs in training domain, channel and resolution; public DeepJSCC also uses
CBR $0.16256$. Either codec would require newly trained, compatible diffusion
priors. Complete published CDDM/ICDM systems are not reproduced.

Development uses 64 validation pairs and seed 1700. For independent
confirmation, we use 256 test pairs, seeds 2700/2701, SNR 20 dB, and nominal SINRs
$\{-7,-4,0,4,7,10\}$ dB. Sixteen 64-symbol blocks have stationary,
alternating $(0.25,1.75)$, or burst $(0,2)$ relative interference powers.
These 18 conditions give 9,216 frames per receiver. We run confirmation only
after freezing the policy. File hashes exclude overlap with development,
pressure tests, and
200 previously evaluated pairs; independence is at image-pair, not identity,
level. The power grid is $\{0,0.1,0.2,0.35,0.5,0.75,1,1.5,2,3,4,8,10^6\}$;
the heterogeneity grid is $\{0.05,0.1,0.2,0.4,0.8,1.6\}$, giving 26 energy-only
and 2,028 two-feature rules across both assignment directions.

We compare with direct decoding, Gaussian reception using $N_0$ alone,
blind Gaussian reception, initialization-only and calibrated joint sampling,
and the selector. The same-gate control uses direct decoding for
$\widehat P\le0.15$ and calibrated joint sampling otherwise. Its outputs and
component times are reconstructed from paired branches, excluding gate
overhead. Development also tests coupled feedback and a Gaussian receiver
given nominal disturbance power, a privileged diagnostic. All SwinJSCC variants
share received tensors and sampler seeds; external codecs share image pairs,
conditions and channel seeds but generate their own codewords.

Measurements use float32, batch size one, TF32 disabled, four CPU threads, and
an RTX 4090. Synchronized receiver time includes selection, sampling and
decoding, excluding encoding, metrics and loading. Function evaluations (NFE)
count calls to both priors.
Quality metrics are per-image PSNR and MSE, LPIPS-VGG v0.1 \cite{zhang2018lpips},
and four-scale MS-SSIM \cite{wang2003msssim}, averaged arithmetically after RGB
clipping to $[0,1]$; four scales fit the image resolution. Paired 95\% intervals
use 5,000 bootstrap resamples of image pairs, retaining both seeds and all
conditions. A harmful decision loses more than 0.5 dB against its comparator.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:discussion}
In this section, we discuss the scope of the findings and conclude the study.
The comparisons support adapting the disturbance model within a fixed
receiver stack. They do not establish a universal preference for either
receiver: harmful decisions persist, and shifted blocks nearly remove the
advantage over Gaussian denoising. The energy statistics provide a validated
decision rule for this population, rather than a physical identification of
the interferer.
